<!--meta Post 4 – Exploring nature (EN) -->
Nature is a vast “classroom”, full of surprises. A falling leaf, a snail inching along, a cloud shaped like a rabbit – any of these can spark a “what’s that?” from a child. When parents answer in English and explore alongside their children, language becomes tied to vivid sensory experiences. This post suggests ways to organise nature activities in English, from the park round the corner to a small balcony.

## Why is nature such a good setting for learning English?

- **It sparks curiosity.** Children naturally want to know the names, colours and shapes of what they see.
- **It engages all the senses.** Children look, listen to birdsong, touch tree bark and smell flowers – each sense becomes an “anchor” for a new word.
- **It is always changing.** The weather, the seasons and the plants change over time, giving you reasons to come back and talk about something new.
- **It gets children moving.** They learn while walking, running and jumping, which suits the need for movement at this age.

## What to prepare before heading out

You don’t need expensive equipment. A cloth bag or small box for “treasures”, a notebook and a few coloured pencils are plenty. Before you go, choose one **language theme** for the outing – such as colours, shapes or sizes – so your child isn’t overwhelmed with new words.

:::callout sun
**Safety first:** Always supervise your child, don’t let them put leaves, berries or insects in their mouth, wash hands after exploring, and watch out for thorny plants or insects that may sting. Choose a safe spot, away from busy roads or unfenced ponds and lakes.
:::

## Six nature activities in English

### 1. Nature scavenger hunt – Hunting for nature’s treasures

Prepare a picture list of 5–6 things to find: a yellow leaf, a round stone, a flower, a stick, a feather. When your child finds one, they say its name and one thing about it out loud.

:::phrases
- I found a leaf!
- It’s yellow and big.
- Look at this stone. It’s round.
:::

### 2. Big or small? – Sorting by size

Bring the “treasures” home and sort them into two groups: big and small, or long and short. Your child practises sorting and comparing while using describing words.

:::phrases
- This leaf is big.
- This one is small.
- Which one is longer?
:::

### 3. Leaf rubbing – Printing leaf patterns

Place a leaf under a thin sheet of paper and gently rub over it with a wax crayon to bring out the pattern of the leaf. As you go, name the parts of the leaf and its colours together.

:::phrases
- Rub, rub, rub!
- I can see the lines.
- My leaf is green.
:::

### 4. Bug hotel – Watching little creatures

Find a corner of the garden or a flower bed and watch (without catching) ants, ladybirds and snails together. Describe how they move.

:::phrases
- Look! An ant.
- It’s small and black.
- The snail is slow.
- Where is it going?
:::

### 5. Weather journal – A daily weather diary

Each day, your child draws a weather symbol on a small chart and says the matching sentence. At the end of the week, count together how many sunny days and rainy days there were.

:::phrases
- It’s sunny today.
- It’s rainy.
- How many sunny days?
:::

### 6. Grow a plant – Growing something together

Plant a bean seed in a plastic cup with damp cotton wool and put it on the windowsill. Each day, look at it, water it and talk about how it has changed. This is a longer-running activity that helps your child use the same set of words again and again over many days.

:::phrases
- Water the seed.
- It’s growing!
- Look, a leaf!
- It’s taller today.
:::

## Suggested vocabulary by theme

| Theme | Suggested words |
|---|---|
| Plants | tree, leaf, flower, grass, seed, stick |
| Small creatures | ant, bee, butterfly, snail, bird, ladybird |
| Weather | sunny, rainy, cloudy, windy, hot, cold |
| Colours | red, yellow, green, brown, blue, orange |
| Describing words | big, small, long, short, soft, rough |

There’s no need to teach the whole table in one go. Focus on just 3–5 words each time.

## What if there’s no park nearby?

Nature can start on a balcony or a windowsill: a small potted plant, a bowl of water for watching raindrops, or simply looking out of the window and talking about the sky. Fruit and vegetables in the kitchen are “nature in miniature” too: pumpkin seeds, vegetable stalks or a carrot cut in half can all become something to explore.

## In summary

Exploring nature in English helps children learn the language while developing their observation skills, their curiosity and a love for the world around them. What matters most is not how many words your child learns in one outing, but the joy of discovering something new together with you.

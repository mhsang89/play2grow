<!--meta Post 6 – Technology and children (EN) -->
Videos, English-learning apps and video games are becoming ever more common. Many parents wonder: how much time learning English on devices is reasonable for my child? And how can technology genuinely support learning rather than taking over their playtime? This post brings together published recommendations and suggests practical ways to strike a balance.

## What do leading organisations recommend?

### World Health Organization (WHO)

In 2019, the WHO published guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5. According to these guidelines:

- **Under 1 year:** screen time is not recommended.
- **1 year old:** sedentary screen time is not recommended.
- **2–4 years:** sedentary screen time should be no more than 1 hour a day; less is better.

The WHO also encourages replacing time spent sitting in front of a screen with activities such as reading and storytelling with a caregiver.

### American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP)

The AAP recommends avoiding digital media for children younger than 18–24 months, other than video-chatting with family. For children aged 2–5, it suggests limiting use to about 1 hour a day of high-quality content, with parents watching together to help their child understand what they see. For older children, the AAP encourages families to make a media use plan that suits them, making sure it doesn’t get in the way of sleep, physical activity and other important activities.

:::callout mint
The recommendations above are general reference information from health organisations and do not replace your doctor’s advice for your own child. Recommendations may be updated over time; please check the latest versions on these organisations’ official websites.
:::

## How can technology help with learning English?

When chosen and used well, technology can:

- **Provide good-quality, varied listening** through songs, stories with audio and videos.
- **Make it quick to look up** how words are pronounced and what they mean.
- **Boost motivation** with lively pictures and sounds.
- **Support parents** in finding activity ideas and suitable materials.

Even so, technology struggles to replace two-way interaction with real people. Young children in particular need to be talked with, to get responses and to use language to do things that matter to them – something that watching a screen alone rarely offers.

## Five principles for a healthy balance when learning English with devices

### 1. Watch together, talk together

Rather than leaving your child to watch alone, sit with them and pause to ask: “What’s that? What colour is the car?” Chatting about what you’re watching turns passive viewing into time spent interacting.

### 2. From the screen to real life

Let each piece of digital content lead to a real activity. Watched a video about animals? Copy how the animals move together. Listened to a song about fruit? Head to the market together and look for those fruits.

### 3. Choose content with purpose

Go for content that suits your child’s age, has a slow pace, few adverts and clear learning goals. Limit content that autoplays one video after another, which makes it hard for children to stop.

### 4. Set clear rules for the whole family

Agree on when and for how long devices are used, for example: no devices at mealtimes or before bedtime. Rules are easier to stick to when the adults follow them too.

### 5. Put first the things that can’t be replaced

Sleep, physical activity, free play and time chatting as a family should come first. Device time should be fitted in once these needs have been met.

## What about AI in children’s education?

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools are appearing more and more in learning apps, from suggesting lessons to speaking-practice conversations. When considering an AI-based product for your child, it helps to ask a few questions:

- What exactly does the AI feature do? Is it described clearly, without exaggeration?
- What data does the product collect about your child (voice, images, conversation content)? Where is it stored, and who can access it?
- Are there ways for parents to monitor and control it?
- Is AI-generated content checked to make sure it is suitable for children?
- Is the amount of use in line with the recommendations for your child’s age?

AI can be a useful supporting tool, but as with any technology, its value depends on how it is designed and how it is used.

## In summary

Technology is not the “enemy” of learning, but nor is it the only answer. When parents actively choose content, use it together with their children and always connect the screen to real experiences, devices can become a helpful part of a child’s English-learning journey.

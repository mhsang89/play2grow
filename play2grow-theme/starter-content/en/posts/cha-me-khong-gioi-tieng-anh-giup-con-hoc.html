“My pronunciation isn’t perfect – will my child pick up mistakes from me?” “I only know a few basic English sentences, so how can I help?” These are very common worries among parents. The good news is that you don’t need to be fluent in English to support your child. What parents bring matters in other ways – and sometimes not being “good at it” yet can even turn out to be an advantage.

What parents really bring

When learning a new language, children need several things: good-quality language to listen to, chances to use it, the motivation to keep going and a safe environment in which they feel brave enough to try. Parents don’t have to be the model of correct English – teachers, audiobooks, songs and good-quality materials can fill that role. But parents are best placed to:

  • Create opportunities for their child to use English in everyday life.
  • Nurture motivation through interest and encouragement.
  • Build habits that are regular and consistent.
  • Set an example of being open to learning.

“Learning together” rather than “teaching”

When a parent happily says, “I don’t know this word either – let’s look it up together!”, the child learns an important lesson: not knowing is normal, and finding out is fun. You can:

  • Look up words together in a dictionary with audio, then listen and repeat.
  • Learn a new English song together each week.
  • Let your child “teach” you what they learnt in class – children love this role, and explaining things again helps them remember more deeply.

Will my pronunciation lead my child astray?

Children learn pronunciation from many different sources: teachers, songs, videos and stories with audio. When children hear a wide range of good-quality pronunciation, occasionally hearing a parent pronounce something imperfectly is not usually a big problem. Here’s what you can do:

  • With new words, listen to the pronunciation together from a reliable source (an audio dictionary, a song) before practising together.
  • Stick to short, familiar phrases you already feel confident with.
  • Don’t be afraid of making mistakes. Your relaxed attitude gives your child the courage to speak, too.

Eight practical things you can start today

  1. Choose 5 English phrases for the whole family to use every day – Good morning, Thank you, Let’s go, Well done, Good night.
  2. Stick English labels on things around the house.
  3. Play children’s songs in English during playtime or on the go, and sing along with your child.
  4. Read bilingual picture books together, or “read the pictures” in wordless books.
  5. Play simple language games such as Simon says or I spy.
  6. Ask your child about their lessons with a simple question: “What did you learn today?”
  7. Watch good-quality English content together and chat about it, rather than leaving your child to watch alone.
  8. Notice and praise effort, even when your child manages just one word.

Things to avoid

  • Comparing your child with others. Every child has their own pace.
  • Turning every moment into a test. Constantly asking “What’s this in English?” can make children feel under pressure.
  • Correcting harshly. Instead, repeat the correct sentence naturally as part of your reply.
  • Expecting quick results. Learning a language is a long journey; many children understand a great deal before they start speaking.

When should you look for extra support?

If your child shows signs of delayed language development in their home language as well, or you have concerns about their hearing or speech, please seek advice from a health professional or a speech and language therapist. In these cases, learning an additional language should be adjusted according to professional advice.

In summary

You don’t need to be an English teacher to help your child learn better. Being there, a spirit of learning together and small daily habits are a precious gift that no one else can replace. Start with the simplest phrases, and enjoy the journey together with your child.

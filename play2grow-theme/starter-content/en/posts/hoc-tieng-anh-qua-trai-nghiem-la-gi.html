When parents hear “learning English through experience”, many picture field trips or outdoor lessons. In reality, an experience can be far simpler: a meal, a game, a moment spent watching the rain together. This article explains what experiential learning is, why the approach suits young children, the most common misconceptions and how you can start right away at home.

What is experiential learning?

Experiential learning is a process in which the learner does something concrete, then observes and reflects on what they have just done, draws new understanding from it and tries applying it in the next situation. The educational researcher David A. Kolb organised this idea into a cycle in his book Experiential Learning, published in 1984, and that cycle is still widely used across many fields of education today.

Applied to children learning English, this means the language isn’t “taught” in isolation; it appears inside a meaningful activity. A child doesn’t learn the word apple by copying it out ten times in an exercise book, but by holding an apple, taking a bite, comparing it with an orange and saying “I like apples!”.

Why does this approach suit young children?

Children learn with their whole body and all their senses

Young children explore the world by looking, listening, touching, smelling, tasting and moving. A new word linked to several senses has many “anchors” to help a child remember it. The Total Physical Response method developed by James J. Asher rests on a similar idea: learners hear an instruction and carry it out with an action – “stand up”, “jump”, “touch your nose” – before they have to speak themselves.

Context helps children understand without translation

When an adult says “It’s hot!” while touching a cup of warm milk, the child understands the sentence straight away from the situation. Clear context helps children guess meaning, make their own connections and gradually develop a “feel” for the language, rather than translating word by word into their home language.

Children have a real reason to communicate

In a shop role-play, a child needs to say “Can I have a banana?” to get the banana. Language becomes a tool for reaching a goal, not just the answer to a teacher’s question. This is also the spirit of task-based language teaching.

Play is how children naturally learn

Respected organisations such as UNICEF and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) have emphasised the role of play in children’s development, including language development and social skills. When English appears in a game, children take part because they are interested, not because they are told to.

Common misconceptions

“Experiential learning is just play, not learning”

Purposeful play is still learning. The difference lies in the adult knowing the language goal of the activity – for example, focusing today on colours or on the sentence pattern “Can I…?” – and weaving it skilfully into the game.

“You need a classroom and a native-speaker teacher to do it”

Experiences can happen right in the kitchen or the living room. What matters is situations that repeat, a few simple sentence patterns and a patient adult.

“Experiential learning can replace grammar, reading and writing”

No. Experiential learning helps children use language naturally, but skills such as reading, writing, accurate pronunciation and grammar still need to be built systematically, especially as children get older. The two ways of learning complement each other.

“After a few sessions, my child will be speaking”

Acquiring a language is a long process. Many children spend quite a while listening and understanding before they feel confident enough to speak. Consistency matters more than intensity.

What does an experiential activity look like?

Example

Making orange juice together

Language goal: names of objects (orange, cup, spoon, water), action verbs (cut, squeeze, pour, stir) and the sentence “I like it!”.

  1. Observe: Put an orange on the table. Ask your child: “What’s this? What colour is it?” Let them hold it, smell it and roll it around.
  2. Explore: Cut the orange in half and show your child the inside. “Look! It’s juicy.” Count the pips together in English.
  3. Use: Squeeze the orange together, pour the juice into a cup and stir. At each step, say the verb out loud – “Squeeze! Pour! Stir!” – and invite your child to repeat it.
  4. Retell: While drinking, ask “Do you like it?”. That evening, ask your child to tell a family member: “We made orange juice!”

The activity takes only about 15 minutes, yet your child has heard and used words linked to a real experience dozens of times. Remember that any steps involving a knife should always be done by an adult.

Getting started at home: five simple principles

  1. Choose an activity your child already enjoys. Don’t invent a new activity just to learn English; add English to what your child is already doing.
  2. Keep the goals small. Just 3–5 words or 1–2 sentence patterns each time.
  3. Say little, repeat often. Use the same sentence many times during the activity and over the following days.
  4. Accept every kind of response. A nod, or a word in your home language mixed in, is still a sign that your child understands.
  5. Stop while your child is still having fun. A positive experience makes them want to come back next time.

Conclusion

Learning English through experience isn’t a complicated set of course books; it’s a way of seeing things: every moment of the day can become a chance for children to meet and use the language. When parents observe, explore and chat together with their children, English gradually becomes a natural part of the child’s world.

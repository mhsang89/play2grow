Parents are fellow explorers – you don’t need to be a teacher

Many parents hesitate to support their child’s English because they worry about their pronunciation or limited vocabulary. In fact, what children need from parents is not a perfect lesson but time, attention and a shared spirit of learning. When you happily look up a new word with your child or have a go at an English sentence, your child learns that learning is normal and fun.

A few principles make supporting your child easier:

  • Follow your child’s interests. Dinosaurs, fire engines or dolls? Start right there.
  • Say less, repeat more. One short phrase used again and again in different situations is worth more than ten new sentences a day.
  • Praise effort, not results. “You had a go – well done!” is more encouraging than “That’s correct.”
  • Don’t force it. If your child doesn’t want to speak today, keep playing and using English naturally; they are still listening and taking it in.

Keeping a positive learning habit

Consistency matters more than intensity. A few tips for the long run:

  1. Choose a regular “English time” – for example 10 minutes after dinner – that the whole family looks forward to.
  2. Keep activities short and stop while your child is still enjoying them. That way they’ll want to play again next time.
  3. Record the journey. A small notebook with pictures and favourite new words helps your child see their own progress.
  4. Change the setting. Home today, the park at the weekend, the supermarket – each place brings new words.
  5. Be patient with the “silent period”. Many children listen for quite a while before they feel confident to speak; that doesn’t mean they aren’t progressing.

Balancing screen time and off-screen activities

Videos, apps and games can be useful sources of English, but they cannot replace real experiences and interaction with adults. Organisations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend limiting screen time for young children and choosing high-quality content, ideally watched together with an adult.

Some simple ways to keep a balance:

  • Watch with your child and talk about the content instead of leaving them to watch alone.
  • Turn what your child has watched into a real activity: after a video about cats, draw a cat together, copy its sounds and name its body parts.
  • Set clear rules about when and how long devices are used – for the whole family.

Questions to ask when choosing a class for your child

Whether you choose Play2Grow or another centre, these questions can help you make the right decision:

  • Which age group and level is the class for? Does it suit my child?
  • What will my child actually do in each lesson or activity?
  • How big is the class? Are the teachers experienced with this age group?
  • What information does the centre collect about my child? Does it take photos or videos of students, and how are they used?
  • How are parents kept informed about their child’s learning?
  • Are fees, and the policies for pausing or stopping lessons, clear?

What does learning through experience mean?

Experiential learning means doing something concrete first, then noticing, reflecting and discovering something new from what you have just done. For English, this means children meet the language inside a real activity – cooking, playing a game, going for a walk, making a craft – and use English to understand, ask, describe or retell that activity.

The idea is not new. It draws on approaches that have been widely studied and used in education: learning through play in the early years, teaching language through meaningful tasks, and combining words with physical actions. Play2Grow selects these ideas and places them in the everyday life of children and families in Vietnam.

The four rhythms of a language experience

An experiential English activity usually moves through four rhythms. They don’t have to follow a strict order, but when all four are present the language has more chances to “stick”.

  1. Notice – Children look at, listen to and touch real things. The adult names and describes them briefly: “Look! A snail. It’s slow.”
  2. Explore – Children ask questions, experiment and compare. Curiosity leads the way: “Is it big? Is it small?”
  3. Use – Children use words and phrases to get something done: choosing food, taking turns, giving directions, presenting something they made.
  4. Share – Children retell or show someone else what they did. Saying it again helps them organise the language and remember it longer.

Examples: English in familiar situations

The examples below are activity ideas for parents to try at home. They illustrate the Play2Grow approach; they are not a description of the content of a specific class.

Exploring nature

A walk in the park can become an English “treasure hunt”: find a yellow leaf, a round stone, a small flower. Each time they find something, children say its name and one thing about it.

  • I found a leaf!
  • It’s yellow.
  • It’s round.
  • Look at this!

Things around the house

Stick small labels with English names on familiar objects: door, window, table, chair. Every time you pass by, touch the object and read the label aloud together. After a few days, remove the labels and see how many your child remembers.

  • Where’s the chair?
  • It’s here.
  • Open the door, please.

Playing shop

Put a few toys, fruit or household items on a table and use paper for money. One person is the shopkeeper, the other the customer. This role-play gives children a clear reason to use English: to get the thing they want.

  • Can I have an apple, please?
  • How many?
  • Here you are.
  • Thank you!

Creative activities

When children draw, model clay or make crafts, ask about colours, shapes and the story behind their work. At the end, invite your child to give a short “presentation” in English about what they made.

  • What colour is it?
  • This is my rocket.
  • It’s big and red.

Language games

Familiar games like “Simon says”, “I spy” or guessing what’s in a bag give children a reason to listen carefully and speak clearly. Simple rules let them focus on the language without feeling overwhelmed.

  • Simon says touch your nose.
  • I spy something blue.
  • Is it a ball?

Everyday conversations

Saying good morning, asking how someone feels, wishing good night – short phrases repeated every day are a gentle way to make English part of family life.

  • Good morning!
  • How are you today?
  • I’m happy.
  • Good night!

What this approach does well – and its limits

No single method suits every child in every situation. Parents should have a balanced view of what learning through experience can and cannot do.

What is the parent’s role?

In experiential learning, parents don’t need to act as teachers. A more important role is being a fellow explorer: setting up situations, sparking questions, listening and encouraging. Even if your own English is limited, looking up words together, listening to pronunciation together and trying to speak together sets a great example of a learning mindset.

Background reading

Play2Grow’s approach builds on widely published educational ideas. Some foundational references for parents who want to read more:

  • The experiential learning cycle – David A. Kolb, Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development (1984).
  • Total Physical Response – developed by James J. Asher, combining spoken language with physical actions.
  • Learning through play – UNICEF, Learning through play (2018); American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) clinical report The Power of Play (2018).
  • Task-based language teaching – an approach in which learners use language to complete a meaningful task.

These references are listed as background to the ideas; they are not reviews or endorsements of Play2Grow.
